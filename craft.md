# Craft, case by case

## Composition and explanation

First ask what the subject of this shot is, how big the screen is, what must be read, and what is different
before and after the change. Design key frames at the real frame size, to catch small text and crowding early.
"This button in the right half of the screenshot" guides a build better than "a stunning tech feel".

Keep style and composition apart: one set of colours, type and material can take different shot sizes, layouts
and motion. Mixing styles is a legitimate storytelling choice, but what each object is and what it stands for must
stay clear. Do not make every shot the same template just to keep things uniform.

Gather the real material before designing; interfaces, product shapes and how data was counted are facts too.
When something is missing, mark the gap; never fake an interface to show an operation that did not happen.

## Why things move

The most useful motion can be named with one verb: gather, split, align, connect, filter, reveal. Understanding
"what changed" should come mostly from the object changing, not from narration explaining an unrelated animation.

Carrying one object through the film saves the viewer from recognising things anew. Keep a hero across the film
only when the story needs it; "there must be a hero" must not become a mascot bolted onto every video.

A quick art piece can live on one main action plus a loop taken from its material: stars flowing, ink spreading,
colour blocks overprinting. A story reaction, settled text, reading a chart can be completely still. The amount
of motion is a diagnostic hint, not a quality score.

A transition can come from the next scene's signature material (a colour dot growing to fill the frame, paper
unfolding, a line extending), or simply be the camera moving through one world. Use a hard cut when it has a clear
rhythmic or semantic job.

## Right, and still flat

A film can say only true things in the right order and still watch like slides. The fault is rarely "not enough
motion"; it is that the thing that carries the meaning never does anything. Three cases, each in three states.

**A process: one change goes from request to live.**
- *Weak:* a title "How a change ships", then five cards that slide in one after another, each a step. The cards move;
  the change does not exist.
- *Right, still flat:* one card travels along a line and stops at each step, the same distance, the same pause, the
  same ease, five times. The object is right and nothing is learnt after the second stop.
- *Works:* the card moves fast through what runs by itself and is held where a person must decide; the hold is the
  point, so it gets the time and the only accent. The fourth and fifth steps take half the time of the first,
  because the viewer can read the grammar by then.

**A number: 96 of 1,000 are still here on day 90.**
- *Weak:* "9.6 %" counts up beside a bar chart that grows.
- *Right, still flat:* a thousand small dots, of which 96 change colour. It is to scale, and at film size it reads as
  a texture.
- *Works:* fifty large marks with "1 mark = 20 people" on screen; they leave in three waves the viewer can count, and
  the five that remain move to the centre and are held alone. The size of a mark is chosen for the frame, the unit
  for the truth.

**A claim in words: "Fewer no-shows this month."**
- *Weak:* the line fades up, centred, and waits.
- *Right, still flat:* each word rises in turn on the beat, all with the same rise and the same gap. It is tidy and
  has no stress.
- *Works:* "Fewer no-shows" lands and holds; the frame changes for "this month" (a band crosses, the ground turns),
  so the second half arrives as an event; then nothing moves for a full second. One stress, one change of state, one
  hold.

In all three the last state does less, not more: it gives the time and the one change to the place the meaning is.

**From what you see to what to change**

| On the sheet or in the render | Usually the cause | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Every scene enters, waits, leaves | scenes are pages; nothing is carried across | keep one object or the stage and change its state; the next scene starts inside the last one's end |
| The same move and pause, again and again | each step given equal weight | the first time full, the later ones shorter; hold only where the meaning is |
| The frame drifts or zooms slowly while nothing happens | the camera is covering for an empty scene | cut the scene's length or give the subject its change; a still frame that is being read needs no drift |
| A screenshot sits for seconds | the screen is shown, not used | crop to the control, do the action, show its result; or cut to one second |
| Motion everywhere, and the eye has nowhere to go | several layers move at once | one layer moves at a time; the rest hold or dim |
| Text and picture say the same thing twice | the caption narrates the animation | let the picture do it and cut the words, or keep the words and still the picture |
| It is correct and nobody would remember a frame | no one picture was designed | choose the frame the film turns on, build it first at full size, and give it the biggest sound |
| The three directions look alike | the method's defaults chose, not the material | start each from a different thing the subject has: its interface, its number, its sentence |

Do not fix a flat film by adding: more elements, a faster cut and a busier ground all leave the cause in place.

## A product on screen: what the well-made films do

From 161 published product films (99 demos, 30 launch films, 20 announcements, 12 brand films), each measured and
looked at frame by frame; 43 looked like top studio work and 39 looked weak. The two groups say much the same
things. They differ in how the product is put in the frame:

| | Best-made | Weak |
|---|---|---|
| The camera pushes in | 74 % | 23 % |
| The camera never moves | 47 % | 92 % |
| One element changes into, or carries into, the next scene | 77 % | 23 % |
| Scenes join by a fade | 16 % | 54 % |
| A single component lifted out and shown alone | 56 % | 31 % |
| The real screen shown whole (a screenshot, a recording) | 30 % | 72 % |
| Share of the film with interface on screen | 55 % | 80 % |

What the weak ones have in common, in order of how often: the whole window small in the frame with text nobody can
read; grey text on a grey or white ground; seconds of watching a cursor travel or a paragraph being typed; no
headline, no name, no ending; headline, screen, headline, screen.

**Show the part, at film size.** The viewer reads a film from a phone's width away.

- **Lift it out.** The control, row, card or panel that this beat is about is on screen alone, or with the rest
  dimmed far back: 20–40 % of the frame's width for a control, more for a panel. The whole window appears for a
  second or two to say where we are, then the film goes back in. Three sizes in a steady rhythm work well: one
  control filling the frame, the whole window, a wall of results.
- **Rebuild at film weight.** A rebuilt interface keeps the product's words, order, colours and shapes, and is drawn
  for the film: text the viewer must read at 32 px or more on a 1080-high frame (40 on a tall one), the product's
  pale greys darkened until they pass 4.5:1, hairlines thickened to be seen, chrome that plays no part left out
  (not faded: a faded panel is still clutter). Say in `sources.md` that it is redrawn. When the user says the screen
  must stay exactly as it is, magnify the whole thing instead and change nothing in it.
- **No empty frame.** At any moment the subject and the words about it cover a real share of the frame. A small
  window in a field of white, or a canvas with one small box on it, is an empty frame: push in until the thing
  being done fills it.
- **Waiting is cut or turned into a beat.** Typing longer than about a second and a half, a cursor crossing the
  screen, a load: speed it up, cut across it, or make it a card (the box closes into a pill with one verb on it).
  Show the click and its result; the time between is not content.

**Join the scenes.** A cut is fine (three films in four use them); what the best ones add is a thread.

- **One thing carries through**: a pill, a dot, a cursor bar, a colour, a sample document. It is there in the first
  scene and it is what the last scene resolves into. Decide it before the scenes.
- **The next scene starts inside the last one's end**: the end of a bar grows into the next ground; a button becomes
  the panel it opens; a shot ends on a round form and the next begins on one. Use a fade only for an ending.
- **One sample all the way.** The same made-up project, the same document, the same picture goes through every
  feature, so each change is seen against the last.

**Give it a finish.** Correct flat cards on a plain ground read as a mock-up. Choose one and keep it for the whole
film: soft shadows and a lit ground (half of the best-made); depth, with only the part being read sharp; a material
(glass, paper, grain) that the product's main control is made of; the product's own selection colour as the light.
More than one accent colour is fine when each has a job.

**The opening and the end.** Something readable is on screen within half a second. The first two seconds are the
result, the claim in large type, or an object the viewer wants to see resolved; never a form being filled in. A demo
names the product in the first second; a launch film can hold the name back for a third of its length while one
object builds toward it. Most films end on the mark alone, held about a second and three quarters; a line to act on
("available today", an address) is for a launch or an announcement.

**Half of it holds.** In these films about half the time nothing moves (the best-made included), a move lasts about
half a second, and only one move in four starts on a beat. Move to bring something new, then let it be read.

Before building, write the film's shots as a table in the treatment, one row a scene, and check each row against
the above: the subject and its share of the frame · the smallest text the viewer must read, in px · what the camera
does · what carries in from the scene before · what is new for the viewer. A row with "whole window", text under
32 px, "static" three times running, or "fade" between two scenes is a row to change before any code. After the
first sheet, check the same five things on the pictures.

## Camera and pacing

Write each move's target and reason. For information in a screenshot, flat zooming is usually enough; split into
parallax layers only when the depth relation is worth saying. When neighbouring focal points are close, merge or
pan between them; avoid pulling out and pushing back in for nothing.

Frame by first computing the box of the key content, then subtracting the caption area and margins. Whether type
is big enough is checked on the final target screen, not by the font size in code or a fixed ratio.

Use only the weights and styles a font actually has. The engine turns off the browser's synthetic bold and italic,
so `font-weight: 700` on a family with no bold (Instrument Serif has a regular and an italic, nothing heavier) draws
the regular weight, not a smeared fake. For a heavier headline, pick a family that has the weight.

Teaching an operation needs the location and the result to be seen clearly; fast-paced pieces can use quick
push-ins, whip pans or fast scale changes. There is no universal shot length, zoom limit or "slower is safer" rule;
those numbers depend on the material's resolution, the amount of information, the film type and the user's notes.

Emphasis can be a quick start and a firm landing; give springiness only to things that need weight or rebound.
Linear motion is legitimate for constant-speed objects and scrolling backgrounds. No easing curve guarantees a
premium feel.

## Sound and picture

With narration, confirm the track and its word timing first, then design the reveals that must sync. Write a cue
as "when this word is said, the viewer sees the result", not as the start of the motion: if something flies in for
half a second, start it early; the landing is the accent.

Holds are for reading and thinking. When the voice has no breath, slowing the picture cannot create an audible
pause; changing the audio needs the user's permission this time, and the user's own words are never cut or added to
automatically.

## Layers of the build

Concept and timing plan → material and drawing → object animation → camera → captions → mix. Find the layer the
problem is in and change that layer. Weak picture craft does not call for a new timeline; narration that is too
dense does not call for endless camera tweaks.

For a canvas style, first understand how the material is made: stroke layout, colour regions, the layer order of
paper and ink. Cache what is static and let stroke direction evolve continuously; reshuffling randomness every frame
becomes flicker. When a character is placed in, match its light or material, and keep the original image.
